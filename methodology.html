How the homebuilding charts were made

Sources, calculations and limits for “New York built 729,000 homes in the 1920s” and “Almost one in five homes standing today went up in the 1920s.” Updated Nov. 3, 2026. The judgment calls are logged in How we built this.

What each bar counts

YearsWhat the bar showsSource

The board’s yearly counts are homes in newly built buildings that got a certificate of occupancy from the Department of Buildings, or the equivalent from the state’s Empire State Development Corporation or Dormitory Authority. Temporary certificates were added for 2004 and 2005. From 2010 the board uses City Planning’s Housing Database. Conversions and demolitions are not counted, so the bars show new construction, not the net change in the city’s housing.

The city’s decade chart doesn’t say how City Planning defined a completed home before 1960, and the 1920s figures come from different record keepers than the modern ones. Treat comparisons across the century as approximate.

Checks

  • The five boroughs add up to the citywide total in every year from 1960 to 2025.
  • The 2006 and 2026 editions of the board’s table match exactly for every borough and year from 1967 to 1999.
  • The board’s yearly counts add up to within 1.5% of City Planning’s decade totals for every decade since the 1960s. The 1960s match within 0.1%: 368,959 against 369,000.
  • The 1920–1928 apartment counts add up to the printed total of 382,670.
  • The build script stops if any of these checks fails.

The 70,000 line

It is a tenth of the “about 700,000 new homes” the city says it needs over the next decade, in its draft Fair Housing Growth Strategy (Aug. 19, 2026). That is an estimate of need, not a production goal. The draft’s five-year target is 350,000. The final version had not been published as of Nov. 3, 2026.

The shaded tax-break periods

  • 1920s exemption. A 1920 state law (Chapter 949, Laws of 1920) let the city exempt new dwellings from local property tax, but not the land under them, until 1932. The city’s ordinance of Feb. 15, 1921 capped the exemption at $1,000 a room and $5,000 a home. Construction had to start by a deadline; the state’s last extension (Chapter 87, Laws of 1924) set it at April 1, 1925, and it was not extended again. In 1923 the city cut the break for apartment buildings to $15,000 per building. The darker shading marks the window to start a building; the lighter shading marks the years the break kept running. Source: Citizens’ Housing and Planning Council, “How Tax Exemption Broke the Housing Deadlock in New York City” (1960), pages 1-8 to 1-10, 2-32 and 2-44. Unresolved: whether the city formally adopted the final 1925 deadline.
  • 421-a. Enacted 1971 (New York State assessor’s manual); closed to new projects June 15, 2022.
  • 485-x. Enacted April 20, 2024 (city open data description).

The shading shows when each break applied. It doesn’t show how much building each break caused.

The second chart: homes by decade built

Residential units in City Planning’s PLUTO tax-lot file (August 2026 release), summed by each lot’s year built and grouped by decade. Total: 3,736,645 homes; the 20,024 with no year are left out of the bars. The file counts only buildings still standing, so decades whose buildings were since torn down are undercounted. The city estimates the year for many older buildings: 44% of homes are in buildings dated to a year ending in 0 or 5. A building’s year is the year it was built, not when its homes were added or converted.

Still missing

Yearly counts of all homes for 1929–1959, and for 1920–1928 apart from 1927. The best lead is City Planning’s 1972 tabulation “New Dwelling Units Completed 1921–1971 in New York City,” held at the New York City Municipal Archives. The Rent Guidelines Board plots a 1921–2024 yearly line in its introduction booklet but doesn’t publish the numbers; that line was not traced for these charts.
