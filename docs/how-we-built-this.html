How we built this

The steps behind the homebuilding charts and the choices that shape what they show. Sources and definitions are in the methodology.

Steps

  1. We gathered every published count of new homes completed in New York City that we could trace to an official source: City Planning’s decade totals from 1920, the Rent Guidelines Board’s yearly counts from 1960, the board’s record figure for 1927 and the State Board of Housing’s yearly apartment counts for 1920–1928.
  2. We read each figure from the original page and checked it against the totals printed in the same source.
  3. We compared overlapping sources. The board’s yearly counts add up to within 2% of City Planning’s decade totals for every decade since the 1960s, and two editions of the board’s table, published 20 years apart, agree for 1967–1999.
  4. A build script repeats these checks every time the data is updated and stops if any fails.
  5. The charts are drawn directly from the checked data. Every number is available in the data download, with its source.

Key decisions

Decade averages for 1920–1959. No published yearly count of all homes covers these years. Leaving them blank would drop nearly 1.4 million verified homes from the picture, so each year shows its decade’s total divided by 10, in a lighter color. This hides swings within each decade; the 1927 record of 107,185 is shown separately above the 1920s average of 72,900.

Apartment-building counts are optional. The State Board of Housing’s yearly figures for 1920–1928 count only buildings for three or more families, a narrower measure than every other bar. They appear only when the reader turns them on.

No estimates from charts or gaps. The Rent Guidelines Board plots a yearly line back to 1921 but doesn’t publish the numbers, and we didn’t trace values off its chart. We also don’t treat the difference between two sources’ figures for the same year as a count of anything, since the sources may define a home differently.

Two editions of one table. Yearly counts for 1960–1966 come from the board’s 2006 report and those for 1967–2025 from its 2026 report, which carries the latest revisions. The two agree exactly where they overlap.

The 1920s tax break in two shades. New buildings qualified only if construction began by April 1, 1925, but the break on those buildings lasted until 1932. The darker shading marks the window to start building; the lighter shading marks the years the break kept running.

The 70,000 line. The city estimates it needs about 700,000 new homes over the next decade. The line shows that estimate as a yearly pace, in the same unit as the bars.

Homes by decade built. The second chart uses the year built in the city’s tax-lot records, which the city estimates for many older buildings. The 0.5% of homes with no recorded year are left out.

Limits

  • The shading shows when each tax break was in effect. It doesn’t show how many homes were built because of a break.
  • The counts cover new construction. They don’t subtract demolitions or add conversions.
  • The figures are not adjusted for the city’s population.
  • Record keeping changed over the century, so comparisons across distant decades are approximate.
